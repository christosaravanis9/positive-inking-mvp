The Provenance Method

How Positive Inking turns a story, or an image you can't fully explain, into an artist-usable Tattoo Blueprint.

The problem with "tell me what you want"

Most tattoo consultations start by asking what you want to see. Positive Inking starts by asking where it came from — and treats those as two different questions that need two different kinds of honesty.

The provenance principle

Origin almost always exists. Significance often does not — and the two get conflated constantly. Someone who has been drawn to an image for years can usually say exactly when they first saw it, and completely honestly say they don't know why it stuck. Positive Inking's rule is simple: record the first, never manufacture the second.

In practice, that means: when you know why something matters, we capture that meaning as you state it — we don't add to it. When you don't know why, we record the origin as fact (where you saw it, when, in what context) and stop there. We do not infer a psychological reason you never gave us. A recalled sighting is a memory, not a hidden explanation waiting to be uncovered.

Two starting points, one convergence

Positive Inking opens with a single branching question: do you know why this matters, or do you know what you want more than why?

If you know why — a memory, a person, a change you're marking — the process interprets your story into themes and a stated intention, then helps you find the specific visual language for it. If you're drawn to an image without a clear reason, the process records that attraction as provenance: where it came from, not what it "really means." No manufactured backstory gets attached to it.

Both paths converge on the same downstream process: finding concrete visual candidates, resolving composition and artistic treatment, and confirming placement — the same rigor regardless of which door you came in through.

What decides the next question — and what doesn't

Positive Inking is built on one architectural rule: the sequence is deterministic code; only interpretation is delegated to a model. Which question comes next, whether a topic needs revisiting, when enough has been gathered — all of that is decided by plain, testable logic, not by a model's judgment call. The model's job is narrower and more specific: interpret a story into themes, recover provenance without inventing significance, propose visual candidates grounded in what was actually said, and write prose. It does not decide what happens next in your intake.

The Blueprint

Everything converges into a single artist-usable document, the Blueprint — not a mood board, not a vague "I want something meaningful." It has a fixed structure: your story or the image's provenance, your stated intention, the visual direction, confirmed visual subjects with their sourcing, composition, artistic treatment, placement, reference material and its status, an artist brief calibrated to how much creative control you want to hand over, and a readiness assessment.

That last part matters: the Blueprint doesn't claim to be finished until it actually is. Readiness is tracked as five separate components — meaning, visual direction, references, artist discussion, final artwork — each marked independently. A Blueprint can be honestly "ready to bring to a consultation" while still flagging, say, that a reference photo hasn't been provided yet. It doesn't paper over what's still open.

What this isn't

This isn't a generator that hands you a finished design. It doesn't invent a meaning you didn't give it, doesn't treat "I just like it" as an answer that needs fixing, and doesn't produce a print-ready image. It produces a brief a tattoo artist can actually work from — with every recommendation clearly labelled as a recommendation, and every unconfirmed decision left open rather than quietly decided for you.
